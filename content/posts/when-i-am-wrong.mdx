It’s never easy to hear that we are wrong.

Even today, a small mistake can make me feel defensive, embarrassed, and search for excuses.

But over time, I have realised this: the moment we accept our mistake is the exact moment we open the doors for growth

Why do we defend our mistakes?

They are mistakes after all, right?

From evolution itself, our minds are wired to protect our self-image. Admitting a mistake feels like admitting weakness. It feels like losing ground, like losing respect — even in our own eyes.

But that’s the trick our mind plays on us.

The real weakness is not in making mistakes. The real weakness is in refusing to face them.

History teaches the same lesson.

Civilizations rose and fell.

Great men were made — and broken.

The ones that fell were often the ones that tried to create a perfect heaven, or present themselves as godlike figures — flawless and unquestionable.

But those who survived were the ones who admitted they were flawed, incomplete, and kept learning.

The great people we look up to today, the democracy we live under — all were built by people who made mistakes, who got it wrong, but most importantly, accepted it and grew from it.

And if that’s true at the scale of civilizations, it’s true for us too.

Mistakes come in all sizes. Some are tiny — a misstep, a wrong word.

Some are huge — life-changing, painful.

Still, no matter the size, defending a mistake is like stacking stones on top of a crack and pretending it’s fixed. It only makes the fall heavier later.

When we defend mistakes to protect our pride, we end up building a life where each new lie, each new cover-up, is just hiding a growing emptiness inside.

At some point, the whole thing collapses.

Accepting a mistake is not losing.

It is choosing to stop digging deeper into the hole.

It is choosing to climb out.

But there’s another side to this too.

It’s not just about how we accept mistakes.

It’s also about how others point them out to us — and how we point out mistakes to others.

Correction, when given wrongly, becomes an attack.

Correction, when given rightly, becomes a gift.

Let me explain with something simple.

Imagine a boy who buys something from a shop and gets scammed while collecting his change.

When he returns home, his mother finds out.

Now, she could scold him harshly for “losing money” — making him feel stupid, guilty, and small.

Or, she could scold him to teach — “Always check your bill. Always count your change.”

The boy might still feel bad in both cases. But in the second case, the anger is wrapped around a lesson.

The boy doesn’t just feel bad — he understands.

He remembers.

And the next time he’s at a shop, even years later, he will count the money without needing anyone to tell him.

Corrections should aim to build, not break.

When we correct someone, our goal shouldn’t be to shame them.

It should be to show them the better road, even if it hurts a little in the moment.

When others correct us, we should listen not to their anger, but to the lesson hidden inside.

Mistakes are not the enemy.

Pride is.

Denial is.

Mistakes are the price we pay for trying.

The real question is — will we use them to grow, or use them to cover ourselves in fear?

The choice is ours, every single time.
