I owe my mother an apology.

For a long time, I thought she simply didn't understand my world.

Whenever I told her about something happening in my life, there was often advice waiting for me. What I should do. What I shouldn't do. What I should be careful about. What could go wrong.

And like most people trying to figure out adulthood for themselves, I didn't always want advice.

Sometimes I just wanted to tell her something.

Sometimes I already knew the risks.

Sometimes I wanted to make the mistake and learn from it myself.

So somewhere along the way, I created a simple explanation for all of it: Amma is old-school.

She worries too much. She doesn't understand how the world works for me. She grew up differently, and now she's looking at my life through the rules of hers.

Whenever her fears collided with something I wanted to experience, I saw it as restriction.

Why can't you just trust me?

Why does everything have to come with advice?

Why can't you understand that this is my life?

And the strange thing is, I don't think I was entirely wrong.

I still believe that there are parts of my life she may never completely understand. I still believe that I should be allowed to make choices she wouldn't make for me. I still want the freedom to experience life, make mistakes, meet people, take risks and figure things out for myself.

So what changed?

Somewhere along the way, I found myself caring deeply about someone else's life.

And slowly, without realizing it, I started doing something familiar.

I started giving advice.

A lot of it.

When they told me about something they were planning, my mind would immediately start thinking ahead.

Have you thought this through?

Do you know what you're doing?

What if the plan changes?

How are you getting back?

Are you depending on someone else?

Will you actually speak up if you're uncomfortable?

I knew they were capable of making their own decisions. I knew it wasn't my life to control. In fact, I genuinely wanted them to experience life on their own terms.

And yet, knowing all of that didn't make the worry disappear.

That was new to me.

There is a strange helplessness in caring deeply about someone whose life you cannot—and should not—control.

You want them to be free.

But you also want them to be safe.

You want them to make their own decisions.

But some part of you desperately wants to protect them from every consequence you can already see coming.

You know that they need to make their own mistakes.

But if you think you can prevent even one of those mistakes from hurting them, how do you simply stay silent?

And sometimes, all of that comes out as advice.

Sometimes too much of it.

And somewhere in all of this, the parallel became impossible for me to ignore.

I was doing many of the same things I had once complained about.

Giving advice even when it wasn't asked for. Worrying about things that might never happen. Wanting someone to be careful even when I knew they were perfectly capable of taking care of themselves. Being uncomfortable with some of their choices while still knowing that those choices were theirs to make.

And the funny thing is, none of it came from thinking that I knew better than them or that I should decide how they lived their life.

It came from a much simpler place.

I cared about them. A lot.

That's when a lot of my conversations with Amma started making more sense to me.

All those times she told me, "Whatever I say, the only thing I have in my mind is your well-being," I always knew she meant it. I never doubted that my mother loved me.

What I didn't understand was how that love could turn into so much worry, so much advice, and sometimes even fear.

Now I find myself doing the same thing.

And for the first time, I'm not looking at those conversations only as her son.

I'm able to look at them from her side too.

It doesn't mean she was right every time.

It doesn't mean I was wrong every time.

And it certainly doesn't mean that love gives someone the right to control another person's life.

It means that I finally understand the conflict she must have carried.

I want you to live your life.

And at the same time,

I desperately don't want anything to happen to you.

Those two thoughts can exist together.

From the receiving end, concern can sometimes feel like control.

Advice can feel like judgment.

Fear can sound like distrust.

And when you're fighting for your independence, it's very easy to see the person standing in your way and completely miss the person standing there because they love you.

Maybe that's one of the strange things about growing up.

You don't necessarily realise that your parents were right.

You realise that they were human.

They were figuring this out too.

They were trying to protect someone they loved while slowly accepting that this person was becoming an adult whose choices they could no longer make.

And perhaps that's what I couldn't see.

So, Amma, I'm sorry.

Not for wanting freedom.

Not for wanting to experience life.

Not for disagreeing with you.

Not even for making choices you wouldn't have made for me.

I'm sorry for reducing your concern to "You're old-school."

I'm sorry for the times I treated your advice as nothing more than an irritation.

I'm sorry for assuming that because you didn't always understand my choices, you didn't understand me.

And I'm sorry for the times I interpreted your fear of seeing me hurt as simply an attempt to stop me from living.

Maybe one day, the person I care about will roll their eyes at my advice too.

Maybe they'll think I worry too much.

Maybe they'll wonder why I can't just trust them to figure things out.

And when that happens, I think I'll understand you a little more.

Because, strangely enough, I had to love someone this way before I could understand what it felt like to be loved this way.
